A fever is a temporary increase in body temperature that usually signals your immune system is working to fight an infection or other illness. Here are some key points to keep in mind:

| Topic | What to Know |
|-------|--------------|
| **Normal body temperature** | Roughly 36.5 °C–37.5 °C (97.7 °F–99.5 °F) when measured orally. |
| **What counts as a fever** | An oral temperature ≥38.0 °C (100.4 °F) is generally considered a fever. |
| **Common causes** | • Viral infections (e.g., colds, flu) <br>• Bacterial infections (e.g., strep throat, urinary tract infection) <br>• Inflammatory conditions (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis) <br>• Heat exhaustion or overexertion <br>• Certain medications or vaccines |
| **Typical symptoms that accompany a fever** | Sweating, chills, headache, muscle aches, fatigue, loss of appetite, or a general feeling of being unwell. |
| **When to monitor** | • Mild fever (≤38.5 °C/101.3 °F) in healthy adults or children, especially if it’s short‑lived. <br>• Use a reliable thermometer and keep a record of readings. |
| **When to seek medical help** | • Fever ≥39.0 °C (102.2 °F) that lasts more than 48 h. <br>• Fever accompanied by severe headache, stiff neck, rash, breathing difficulty, persistent vomiting, confusion, or seizures. <br>• In infants under 3 months old with any fever. <br>• In older adults or people with weakened immune systems, any fever warrants a check‑up. |
| **Self‑care tips** | • Stay hydrated (water, clear broths, oral rehydration solutions). <br>• Rest and avoid strenuous activity. <br>• Use a cool compress or lukewarm shower to help lower temperature. <br>• Over‑the‑counter antipyretics (acetaminophen or ibuprofen) can reduce fever and discomfort, but follow dosing instructions and consider any medical conditions or medications you’re taking. |
| **What to avoid** | • Over‑cooling (ice baths or very cold showers) can cause shivering, which may raise core temperature. <br>• Ignoring persistent or severe symptoms. |

**Bottom line:** A fever is usually a sign that your body is fighting something. Mild fevers often resolve on their own, but if you’re concerned—especially if the fever is high, persistent, or paired with serious symptoms—contact a healthcare professional promptly.
